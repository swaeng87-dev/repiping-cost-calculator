House Repiping Cost Guide: What You'll Actually Pay for PEX or Copper in 2026

Updated July 2026 · 12 min read

If a plumber has told you it's time to repipe, or you've noticed rusty water, pinhole leaks, or water pressure that seems to drop a little more every year, you're probably wondering one thing above all else: how much does house repiping cost? The honest answer is "it depends," but it depends on a short, predictable list of factors — and once you know them, you can estimate your own project within a few hundred dollars before a plumber ever sets foot in your home. This guide walks through exactly what drives the price, how PEX compares with copper, and how to avoid overpaying.

1. Signs Your House Needs Repiping

Most homeowners don't wake up one day and decide to repipe for fun — something usually prompts the search. Keep an eye out for:

  • Discolored or rusty water, especially right after the water has sat in the pipes overnight.
  • Frequent pinhole leaks appearing in different spots, a classic sign of failing galvanized steel or copper.
  • Steadily dropping water pressure caused by mineral buildup narrowing the interior of old pipes.
  • A home built before the 1990s that still has its original galvanized steel or polybutylene supply lines.
  • Visible corrosion or greenish staining around exposed fittings and valves.
  • Rising water bills with no obvious explanation, which can indicate a slow hidden leak.

If two or more of these sound familiar, it's worth running your numbers through the calculator above rather than paying for leak repair after leak repair.

2. What Actually Drives Your Repiping Cost

Every repiping quote, whether it comes from a calculator or a plumber standing in your basement, is built from the same handful of variables:

Home Size

More square footage means more linear feet of pipe, which is the single biggest cost driver.

Number of Fixtures

Each sink, toilet, tub, and appliance hookup needs its own supply run and connection.

Stories & Layout

Multi-story homes need vertical runs through walls and floors, adding labor time.

Foundation Type

Slab foundations often require overhead routing through the attic, which raises labor cost versus a crawlspace or basement.

Pipe Material

PEX is generally the most affordable, while copper carries a premium in both material and soldering labor.

Wall Type

Drywall is the easiest to open and patch; plaster, tile, and masonry walls add labor and finishing costs.

3. PEX vs. Copper vs. CPVC: Which Should You Choose?

There's no universally "best" pipe material — only the best fit for your budget, water chemistry, and how long you plan to stay in the home. Here's how the three most common options stack up.

Material Relative Cost Typical Lifespan Best For
PEX-A $ (Lowest) 40–50 years Most single-family homes; freeze-prone climates
PEX-B $ (Low) 40–50 years Budget-conscious repipes
CPVC $$ (Moderate) 50+ years Acidic water conditions
Copper (Type L/M) $$$ (Highest) 50–70+ years Long-term durability, resale value, UV exposure

For most homeowners, PEX-A strikes the best balance of cost and performance, which is why it's the default recommendation in the calculator above. Copper remains the choice for buyers who want the longest possible lifespan and don't mind paying more upfront.

5. The Repiping Process, Step by Step

  1. On-site inspection: A plumber confirms pipe material, routing, and access points, and finalizes the quote.
  2. Permit filing: The contractor pulls the required municipal plumbing permit before work begins.
  3. Water shutoff & access cuts: The main water supply is shut off and small, targeted openings are made in drywall or flooring.
  4. Old pipe removal: Failing galvanized, polybutylene, or corroded copper lines are removed or capped.
  5. New pipe installation: New PEX, copper, or CPVC lines are run to every fixture and pressure-tested.
  6. Inspection: A municipal inspector signs off on the completed work before walls are closed.
  7. Patch & restore: Drywall patching, texture matching, and clean-up finish the job.

6. How to Save Money Without Cutting Corners

  • Choose PEX-A over copper unless you have a specific reason to need copper's rigidity or UV resistance.
  • Bundle repiping with water heater replacement if yours is nearing the end of its life, since labor overlaps.
  • Get multiple written quotes that itemize labor, materials, permits, and drywall patching separately.
  • Ask whether a manifold (home-run) PEX system is available; it can simplify future repairs even if it costs slightly more upfront.
  • Schedule during a contractor's off-peak season (typically late fall or winter) when pricing is often more competitive.

7. Permits & Inspections

Nearly every US municipality requires a plumbing permit for whole-house repiping, along with a final inspection before walls can be closed up. A licensed contractor will handle the paperwork, but it's worth confirming that permit costs are included in your quote rather than added afterward. Skipping permits can create real problems later — unpermitted plumbing work is a common issue that surfaces during home inspections and can complicate a future sale.

8. How to Choose the Right Repiping Contractor

  • Confirm the plumber is licensed, bonded, and insured in your state.
  • Ask for a written, itemized estimate rather than a single lump-sum number.
  • Check whether the quote includes a workmanship warranty separate from the manufacturer's pipe warranty.
  • Look for recent local reviews specifically mentioning repiping, not just drain cleaning or repairs.
  • Confirm who is responsible for drywall patching and cleanup so there's no surprise afterward.
